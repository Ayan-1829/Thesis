Designing Your Methodology

Before we start

Why this matters

Your model reaches 94% accuracy. Is that good? Compared with what, on which data, measured how — and would it be 94% again tomorrow?

A methodology answers these questions before you start, so that your result means something. Without it, even a correct result cannot convince an examiner.

This chapter shows how to design a study: choose a design, describe your method, pick fair baselines and the right metrics, and anticipate threats to validity.

Part 1 of 10

What a methodology is

The methodology is the plan that turns your research question into evidence: what data, what method, compared with what, measured how. It must be detailed enough that another student could repeat your study.

  • It follows from the research question — not from the tool you want to use.
  • It is decided before the experiments, then refined.
  • It names its own weaknesses (threats to validity).

A methodology answers

  1. Which research design?
  2. What data, and how is it prepared?
  3. What is the proposed method?
  4. What is it compared with (baselines)?
  5. How is success measured (metrics)?
  6. How is the evaluation set up?
  7. What could make the results wrong?

Part 4 of 10

The experimental pipeline

  • Data: source, size, labels, licence, ethics.
  • Pre-processing: cleaning, normalisation, tokenisation, augmentation — applied the same way to every method.
  • Proposed method: what is new, with a diagram and every setting.
  • Baselines: the methods you compare against.
  • Evaluation: split, metrics, number of runs, statistics.

The proposed method

Describing your proposed method

  • A block diagram of the whole system, then each block in detail.
  • Equations or pseudocode for the new parts; cite the parts you reuse.
  • All hyperparameters and how they were chosen (on validation data).
  • Hardware and software: GPU, library versions, training time.
  • Why each design choice was made — and the alternatives you rejected.
Write it so a classmate could re-implement it without asking you. If something is “standard”, cite the source that defines it.

Baselines

Kind of baselineWhy include itExample
Trivialshows the task is not trivialalways predict the majority class; random
Classica simple, well-known methodlogistic regression or SVM on TF-IDF
State of the artthe method you must beat or matchthe best recent model on this benchmark
Ablation of your own methodshows what each part contributesyour model without the new component
Tune baselines as carefully as your own method, on the same data and split. A weak, untuned baseline makes any method look good — and examiners know it.

Part 9 of 10

Threats to validity

ThreatQuestionMitigation
Internal validityIs the improvement really caused by my method?same data and tuning for all methods; ablation; several seeds
External validityDo results hold beyond this dataset?test on a second dataset or real-world data
Construct validityDoes the metric measure what I care about?choose metrics carefully; add human evaluation
Conclusion validityIs the difference statistically reliable?enough data, variance, significance tests

Every thesis has threats. Naming them, with what you did about them, is a strength, not a weakness.

Part 10 of 10

The research proposal

  1. Title and one-paragraph summary
  2. Introduction: problem, motivation, research questions, objectives
  3. Literature review: key work and the gap (shorter than in the thesis)
  4. Methodology: design, data, proposed method, baselines, metrics, evaluation setup
  5. Expected contributions and risks (with fallback plans)
  6. Timeline (Gantt chart) and resources needed
  7. References

The proposal is a contract with your supervisor and committee: once approved, the methodology is the plan you follow — and changes are discussed, not hidden.

Worked example 1 of 3

Worked example 1

RQ: “Does continued pre-training on code-mixed text improve sentiment classification of Banglish comments?” Design the methodology in six lines.
Show the solution
Design: controlled experiment. Data: 5,000 labelled comments, 70/15/15 stratified split, plus a public Bangla dataset for external testing. Proposed: BanglaBERT with continued pre-training on 1M unlabelled code-mixed comments, then fine-tuning. Baselines: majority class; TF-IDF + logistic regression; BanglaBERT fine-tuned only; multilingual XLM-R. Metrics: macro-F1 (primary), per-class F1. Setup: 5 seeds, mean ± sd, paired test on macro-F1; error analysis of 100 misclassified comments.

Worked example 2 of 3

Worked example 2

A student plans to compare their model with a 2016 SVM baseline only. What do you advise?
Show the solution
Add a trivial baseline (majority class), a strong recent baseline (the current best on that dataset) and an ablation of their own model. Tune all of them with the same budget on the same split. Otherwise the improvement is not convincing.

Worked example 3 of 3

Worked example 3

A cancer-screening model has 98% accuracy on data with 2% positive cases. Which metrics should be reported?
Show the solution
Accuracy is misleading (predicting “healthy” always gives 98%). Report sensitivity (recall), specificity, precision (PPV), F1 or AUC-PR, and the confusion matrix — with sensitivity as the primary metric if missing a cancer is the main risk.

Try each one yourself first, then check the answer.

4. Why is accuracy a poor metric for imbalanced data?
Show the answer
Predicting the majority class gives high accuracy while missing the minority class completely; use macro-F1, recall, balanced accuracy or AUC-PR.
5. Explain external validity with an example.
Show the answer
Whether results generalise beyond the study: a model tested only on lab photos may fail on field photos; testing on a second, real-world dataset checks it.
6. List the sections of a research proposal.
Show the answer
Title and summary, introduction (problem, RQs, objectives), literature review and gap, methodology, expected contributions and risks, timeline, references.

Summary

Summary

  • The methodology turns the research question into evidence and must be repeatable.
  • Choose the design from the question: experiment, design science, empirical study, survey, simulation or case study.
  • Describe the proposed method fully: diagram, settings, choices and alternatives.
  • Compare with trivial, classic, state-of-the-art and ablation baselines, tuned equally.
  • Pick metrics that match the question; accuracy misleads on imbalanced data.
  • Name threats to validity and what you did about them; the proposal fixes the plan.
