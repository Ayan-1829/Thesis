Qualitative and Mixed Methods

When to use qualitative research, interviews, focus groups, observation and diary studies, sampling and saturation, interview guides, transcription, thematic analysis, rigour, surveys, mixed-methods designs and reporting findings.

Before we start

Why this matters

Your app’s logs show that 70% of farmers stopped using it after a week. You have the number. You have no idea why. Twelve conversations would tell you.

Many good theses ask questions about people: why they adopt or reject technology, how they work, what they need. Answering them well takes methods as rigorous as any experiment — just different ones.

This chapter covers interviews, observation and surveys, thematic analysis and how to combine numbers and words in a mixed-methods thesis.

Section 1 of 4

When numbers are not enough

Qualitative research studies experiences, reasons and practices through words, observations and artefacts. It answers how and why questions that a metric cannot: why developers skip tests, how farmers use a weather app, what makes students distrust an AI tutor.

In computer science it is central to HCI, software engineering, ICTD, security (usable security) and computing education — and useful in any thesis that builds something for people.

ApproachAnswersTypical data
Quantitativehow much, how many, which is bettermetrics, measurements, closed survey answers
Qualitativehow, why, what is it likeinterviews, observation notes, open answers, documents
Mixed methodsboth, with each explaining the othera survey plus interviews, an experiment plus observation

Document and artefact analysiscode reviews, forum posts, app reviews, policiesa defined sample
Choose the method from the research question, not from what is easiest. “How do…?” and “Why do…?” questions about people usually need interviews or observation.

Section 2 of 4

Participants and sampling

  • Purposive sampling: choose people who have the experience you study (e.g. students who used AI assistants this term).
  • Aim for variety: years, genders, backgrounds, skill levels.
  • Stop when new interviews add few new ideas (saturation); for a focused question this is often 9–15 interviews.
  • Describe every participant in a table: P1…P12, role, experience — never names.

Before you recruit

  • ethics approval or your department’s process (chapter 9)
  • an information sheet and consent form
  • a plan for storing recordings safely
  • a short pilot interview

Section 2 of 4

Writing an interview guide

  1. Warm-upbackground, easy questions
  2. Main questions4–6 open questions on the research question
  3. Probes“Can you give an example?” “What happened next?”
  4. Close“Is there anything I should have asked?”
Instead of…ask…
Do you find the app useful?Tell me about the last time you used the app.
Don’t you think the forum is confusing?How do you find information on the forum?
Why and how often do you use Git?How do you use Git? (then, separately) How often?

Section 2 of 4

Recording, transcribing and translating

  • Record audio with consent; keep files encrypted and off shared drives.
  • Transcribe verbatim; remove names, places and other identifying details as you go.
  • Interviews in Bangla: analyse in Bangla where possible; translate only the quotes you report, and say who translated them.
  • Automatic transcription (e.g. Whisper) saves time, but check every line — and never upload sensitive recordings to an online service without consent.
  • Keep a research diary: what you noticed after each interview, and how your ideas changed.

Section 3 of 4

Thematic analysis in six phases

  1. 1. Familiariseread every transcript; note first ideas
  2. 2. Codelabel meaningful segments with short codes
  3. 3. Generate themesgroup codes into candidate themes
  4. 4. Reviewcheck themes against the codes and the whole data
  5. 5. Define and namewrite what each theme is and is not
  6. 6. Write uptell the story with quotes

After Braun and Clarke (2006), the most widely used approach for an undergraduate thesis. A free tool such as Taguette or QualCoder, or a spreadsheet, is enough.

Section 3 of 4

From quotes to codes to themes

Quote (anonymised)CodesTheme
“I ask ChatGPT only after I’ve been stuck for an hour.” (P3)AI as last resort; time pressureRules students set for themselves
“If the code runs, I submit it. I don’t always know why it works.” (P7)working code over understandingUnderstanding traded for speed
“My teacher never said what is allowed, so I just don’t mention it.” (P11)unclear rules; hiding useUncertainty about what is allowed
“I rewrite its answer in my own way so I can explain it in the viva.” (P2)rewriting; preparing to explainRules students set for themselves
A theme is a pattern of meaning across participants, not a topic heading. “AI” is a topic; “students set their own rules because the official ones are unclear” is a theme.

Section 3 of 4

Making qualitative work rigorous

Audit trail

Keep the guide, codebook, memos and every version of the themes.

Reflexivity

Write down how your own position (a fellow student, a TA) may shape what people tell you.

Second coder

A peer codes some transcripts; discuss disagreements. Some report Cohen’s κ, others prefer discussion — say which and why.

Negative cases

Look for participants who do not fit a theme and report them.

Thick description

Describe the setting and participants so readers can judge where the findings apply.

Member checking

Show a summary to some participants and ask whether it is fair.

Section 4 of 4

Surveys that measure something

  • Use validated scales where they exist (SUS, NASA-TLX, UMUX); cite them and do not change the wording.
  • One idea per question; neutral wording; a balanced Likert scale (e.g. 1–5).
  • Pilot with 3–5 people; time it; fix unclear questions.
  • Report the response rate and who responded — a convenience sample of friends cannot speak for “Bangladeshi students”.

Analysing Likert answers

Single Likert items are ordinal: report medians and distributions (stacked bars), and compare groups with Mann–Whitney or Wilcoxon tests. Multi-item validated scales such as SUS can be scored and averaged as their authors specify.

Section 4 of 4

Mixed-methods designs

DesignOrderExample
Explanatory sequentialnumbers first, then interviews to explain themLog data shows 60% never open the LMS forum; interviews find out why.
Exploratory sequentialinterviews first, then a survey to measure how common the themes areInterviews with 12 students produce themes; a survey of 300 counts them.
Convergentboth at once, compared at the endA usability test records task times and think-aloud comments together.
Say in the methodology which design you use and where the two strands are combined — mixing is more than having both a survey and interviews.

Section 4 of 4

Reporting qualitative findings

  • One section per theme: define it, explain it, support it with 2–3 short quotes from different participants.
  • Attribute quotes to IDs (P4), never names.
  • Use counts with care (“most participants”, “7 of 12”) — the aim is understanding, not percentages.
  • Discuss transferability: to which people and settings the findings may apply.
  • Put the interview guide and the codebook in an appendix.

A findings sentence

“Students set their own rules for using AI assistants because the official rules were unclear. Seven of twelve described a personal rule, such as asking only after being stuck for an hour (P3) or rewriting every answer so they could explain it (P2).”

Worked example 1 of 2

Worked example 1

Three excerpts from interviews with developers at local start-ups: (a) “We have no time for tests; the client wants features yesterday.” (b) “Tests? Nobody here knows how to write good ones.” (c) “We tried, but the tests broke every week, so we stopped.” Code them and suggest a theme.
Show the solution
Codes: (a) deadline pressure, client priorities; (b) lack of testing skills; (c) brittle tests, abandoned practice. Possible themes: “Testing loses to delivery pressure” (a), and “Testing is attempted but not sustained without skills” (b, c). Check each theme against all transcripts, look for developers who do test, and give each theme a definition before writing it up.

Worked example 2 of 2

Worked example 2

Research question: “Why do students rarely use the university LMS discussion forum?” Design a mixed-methods study.
Show the solution
Explanatory sequential design. Strand 1 (quantitative): LMS logs for one term — share of students who read or post, by year and course (with permission and anonymised). Strand 2 (qualitative): semi-structured interviews with 12–15 students chosen to include readers, posters and non-users; thematic analysis. Integration: use the log patterns to choose interviewees, and use the themes to explain the patterns. Ethics: approval, consent, anonymised data. Output: the reasons, how common the usage patterns are, and design recommendations.

Practice

Practice (1 of 2)

Try each one yourself first, then check the answer.

1. Give two research questions that need qualitative methods and one that needs quantitative methods.
Show the answer
Qualitative: “Why do developers in local start-ups skip unit testing?”, “How do visually impaired students use smartphones for study?” Quantitative: “Does dark mode reduce reading speed on a news app?”
2. What is saturation?
Show the answer
The point at which new interviews add few or no new codes or themes, so more data is unlikely to change the findings.
3. Rewrite the leading question “Don’t you think the app is hard to use?”
Show the answer
“Tell me about the last time you used the app.” followed by probes such as “What was difficult, if anything?”

Practice

Practice (2 of 2)

Try each one yourself first, then check the answer.

4. What is the difference between a code and a theme?
Show the answer
A code labels one meaningful segment of data; a theme is a pattern of shared meaning across many codes and participants that answers the research question.
5. Name three ways to make qualitative analysis rigorous.
Show the answer
An audit trail; reflexive notes; a second coder on part of the data; reporting negative cases; thick description; member checking.
6. In an exploratory sequential design, which comes first?
Show the answer
The qualitative strand (e.g. interviews); its themes then shape a quantitative instrument (e.g. a survey) that measures how common they are.

Summary

Summary

  • Qualitative research answers how and why questions with words, observations and artefacts.
  • Choose the method from the question: interviews, focus groups, observation, diaries or documents.
  • Sample purposively, aim for variety, stop near saturation; get consent and protect recordings.
  • Use open, neutral interview questions with probes; pilot the guide.
  • Analyse with thematic analysis and make it rigorous: audit trail, reflexivity, second coder, negative cases.
  • Combine numbers and words with a named mixed-methods design, and report themes with quotes.
