Selecting and Judging Papers

Screening search results in stages with inclusion and exclusion criteria, the PRISMA flow, judging venues (CORE ranks, journal quartiles, predatory venues) and judging the quality of a paper itself.

Before we start

Why this matters

Your search returned 1,800 results. You have a month. Which 30 do you read?

Selecting papers is a skill: screening in stages with written criteria, judging where a paper was published, and judging the paper itself. Done well, it saves weeks and protects your thesis from weak or fake sources.

This chapter turns a long list of results into a short list you can trust and defend.

Selection is a funnel. Do it in stages, with written criteria, so that the decision for every paper can be explained — and repeated by someone else.

Screening

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Include if…

  • addresses Bangla or Bengali text
  • performs sentiment or emotion classification
  • reports quantitative results
  • published 2018 or later
  • peer-reviewed, or a preprint from a known group

Exclude if…

  • only English or other languages
  • no evaluation (position papers)
  • not available in full text
  • a duplicate or an earlier version of the same work
  • published in a predatory venue

Write the criteria before screening. If you change them, write down when and why.

Judging quality

Predatory journals and conferences

  • Promise acceptance in days, or “guaranteed publication”.
  • Ask for fees before review, or hide the fees.
  • Send flattering spam emails inviting you to submit or to join the editorial board.
  • Have a very broad scope (“engineering, science and management”).
  • Show fake or invented impact factors.
  • Have no real peer review, and editors you cannot verify.
A paper in a predatory venue is weak evidence; publishing your own thesis work there can harm your record. Check with Think. Check. Submit. and ask your supervisor.

Judging the paper

Citations and recency

  • Citation count shows attention, not correctness. Old papers have more; new strong papers have few.
  • Compare a paper with others of the same age in the same field.
  • Seminal papers (the ones that started a line of work) are worth citing even if old.
  • For methods and results, prefer the last 3–5 years; the state of the art moves fast.

Balanced reading list

2–3 seminal papers · 2–3 recent surveys · 10–20 recent papers close to your problem · the papers that introduced your datasets and metrics · a few from your local context.

Worked example 1 of 3

Worked example 1

A search found 412 records (IEEE 140, ACM 72, Scopus 200). 96 were duplicates. Title screening removed 210, abstract screening 70, and full-text reading 14. How many papers are included?
Show the solution
412 − 96 = 316 screened by title; 316 − 210 = 106 abstracts; 106 − 70 = 36 full texts; 36 − 14 = 22 included. Report these numbers in a PRISMA flow diagram.

Worked example 2 of 3

Worked example 2

An email invites you to publish in the “International Journal of Advanced Science, Engineering and Management”, with review in 72 hours and a fee of 150 USD. What do you do?
Show the solution
Treat it as predatory: very broad scope, unrealistic review time, unsolicited email and up-front fee. Check it on Scopus and in the Think. Check. Submit. list, ask your supervisor, and do not submit or cite it as strong evidence.

Worked example 3 of 3

Worked example 3

Paper A (2017, 900 citations) and paper B (2024, 15 citations) both address your problem. Which do you cite?
Show the solution
Both, for different reasons: A as the seminal work that defined the approach, B as the current state of the art and a likely baseline. Citation counts must be compared within the same age.

Practice

Practice (1 of 2)

Try each one yourself first, then check the answer.

1. Name the stages of screening.
Show the answer
Identify, remove duplicates, screen titles, screen abstracts, read full texts, include.
2. Give two inclusion and two exclusion criteria for a review of phishing-detection papers.
Show the answer
Include: detects phishing URLs or pages; reports quantitative evaluation. Exclude: only email spam (not phishing); no evaluation; predatory venue; duplicate version.
3. Where do you check a conference’s rank and a journal’s quartile?
Show the answer
Conference rank: the CORE portal. Journal quartile: SCImago (SJR), Scopus or Web of Science.

Practice

Practice (2 of 2)

Try each one yourself first, then check the answer.

4. List four signs of a predatory venue.
Show the answer
Guaranteed or very fast acceptance; fees before review; spam invitations; very broad scope; fake impact factors; no verifiable editors or peer review.
5. Why is a high citation count not proof that a paper is correct?
Show the answer
Citations measure attention (and age); a paper can be cited often for being criticised or simply for being early. Correctness comes from method and evidence.
6. Can you cite a blog post or ChatGPT as evidence?
Show the answer
No. Use them for understanding or keywords, then find and cite the peer-reviewed source; verify every reference an AI tool gives you, because it can invent papers.

Summary

Summary

  • Screen in stages — duplicates, titles, abstracts, full text — against criteria written in advance.
  • Report the numbers with a PRISMA flow diagram.
  • Judge the venue: CORE rank for conferences, SJR quartile for journals, indexing and peer review.
  • Avoid predatory venues: fast guaranteed acceptance, up-front fees, spam, vague scope.
  • Judge the paper: clear problem, fair baselines, described data, reliable results, reproducibility, honest limitations.
  • Balance seminal and recent work; use preprints with care; never cite AI chatbots or unverified references.
