A worked thesis

This is a mock thesis, written for this guide: the project, the numbers and the comments are illustrative, and the three “papers” in stage 3 are typical examples, not real citations. It follows one student project — sentiment analysis of code-mixed Bangla–English (Banglish) Facebook comments — from a vague idea to the defence. At each stage you see the student’s draft, the supervisor’s comment and the revision. Read the drafts first and try to spot the problem yourself.

Stage 1 · chapter 1, chapter 2

From an idea to a research question

Draft

“I want to do sentiment analysis of Bangla using deep learning.”

Supervisor’s comment

“Which Bangla? Formal news text is well studied. What is new here, what data will you use, and how will you know you succeeded? Bring me a question, not an area.”

Revision

“Does continued pre-training of BanglaBERT on unlabelled code-mixed comments improve sentiment classification of Banglish Facebook comments, compared with fine-tuning alone, measured by macro-F1?” Scope: three classes; 5,000 comments to be labelled; one platform.

Why it changed. The draft named an area and a technique. The revision names the data (Banglish comments), the method (continued pre-training), the comparison (fine-tuning alone) and the outcome (macro-F1) — it can now be answered and checked.

Stage 2 · chapter 3, chapter 4

Searching and screening

Draft

Searched Google Scholar for “Bangla sentiment analysis”; saved the first 30 results.

Supervisor’s comment

“Thirty papers from one query on one site is a sample, not a search. You missed the code-mixed work entirely. Keep a log so you can describe it in the thesis.”

Revision

A search log with three databases (Google Scholar, IEEE Xplore, ACL Anthology), a Boolean string — (“code-mixed” OR “code-switched” OR Banglish) AND sentiment AND (Bangla OR Bengali) — dates and counts; written inclusion and exclusion criteria; 549 found → 61 screened in → 24 read in full → 17 included.

Why it changed. A logged, repeatable search with explicit criteria can be defended in the viva (“how do you know you did not miss important work?”). It also found the code-mixed papers that the first query missed.

Stage 3 · chapter 5

Reading and comparing three papers

Draft

One paragraph summarising each paper, in the order they were found.

Supervisor’s comment

“Summaries do not show me what you learned. Put the papers side by side: what data, what method, what result, and what did each one not do?”

Revision

Literature-matrix rows: Paper 1 (2021) formal Bangla news comments, BiLSTM, F1 0.71, formal script only. Paper 2 (2022) code-mixed hate speech, mBERT, F1 0.76, no sentiment labels. Paper 3 (2023) BanglaBERT on six tasks, strong on formal text, not tested on Latin-script Bangla.

Why it changed. The matrix makes the pattern visible: no paper combines code-mixed Latin-script Bangla, sentiment and a Bangla-specific pre-trained model. That pattern becomes the gap.

Stage 4 · chapter 6

Stating the gap

Draft

“There is very little work on Banglish sentiment analysis, so this research is important.”

Supervisor’s comment

“‘Little work’ is not a gap, and ‘important’ is not an argument. What exactly has not been done, and why does it matter that it has not?”

Revision

“Models pre-trained on formal Bangla perform well on news and reviews [3], and domain-adaptive pre-training improves other languages’ social-media tasks, yet no study has tested whether it helps sentiment classification of Latin-script Banglish, and no labelled Banglish sentiment dataset is public. This thesis addresses both.”

Why it changed. The revision states precisely what is missing, supports each claim with a citation, and ties the gap directly to the research question and the contributions.

Stage 5 · chapter 7

The evaluation plan

Draft

Random 80/20 split; compare BanglaBERT with and without continued pre-training; report accuracy.

Supervisor’s comment

“Accuracy on imbalanced classes will flatter you. Comments from the same page are similar — a random split leaks. Where is a simple baseline? How will you know a difference is real?”

Revision

70/15/15 split, stratified by class and grouped by Facebook page. Baselines: majority class, TF-IDF + logistic regression, BanglaBERT fine-tuned. Metric: macro-F1. Five seeds per model; mean ± sd; paired t-test over seeds plus a bootstrap confidence interval on the test set. Same tuning budget for every model.

Why it changed. Each change answers a likely examiner question: leakage (grouped split), metric choice (macro-F1), whether the task is easy (simple baselines) and whether the gain is real (seeds, test and interval).

Stage 6 · chapter 8

Results, ablation and errors

Draft

“Our model achieved 0.83 macro-F1, outperforming all baselines.” (best seed of five)

Supervisor’s comment

“Reporting your best seed is selective reporting. Show all runs. Which part of the method helps? Where does it still fail?”

Revision

Main table, mean ± sd over 5 seeds: majority 0.21, TF-IDF + LR 0.68 ± 0.01, BanglaBERT 0.78 ± 0.02, ours 0.81 ± 0.01 (+3.0 points; 95% bootstrap CI 1 to 5 points; paired t-test p = 0.004). Ablation: without continued pre-training 0.781. Error analysis of 100 errors: sarcasm 31, mixed sentiment 24, spelling variants 18, label noise 9.

Why it changed. The honest mean over seeds is lower than the best seed, but it is defensible. The ablation shows which part matters; the error analysis explains the remaining failures and gives future work (sarcasm).

Stage 7 · chapter 9

Ethics and data

Draft

Comments scraped with a script and shared in full on GitHub.

Supervisor’s comment

“These are people’s words, with their names. Check the platform’s terms, remove identifiers and think about what you can release.”

Revision

Comments collected from public pages with permitted tools; usernames and links removed before labelling; stored on a university drive; only text and labels released, for research use; AI-tool use (grammar checking only) declared.

Why it changed. Public is not the same as free to republish. The revision protects the people in the data and keeps the dataset usable for others.

Stage 8 · chapter 10

The abstract and the final claim

Draft

“In this thesis we propose a novel deep-learning approach that achieves state-of-the-art performance on Banglish sentiment analysis.”

Supervisor’s comment

“Novel? State of the art compared with what? An abstract needs the problem, what you did and the actual numbers.”

Revision

“Social-media comments in Bangladesh often mix Bangla and English in Latin script. Models trained on formal Bangla perform poorly on such text, and no labelled dataset exists. We labelled 5,000 Facebook comments and compared six models. Continued pre-training of BanglaBERT on one million unlabelled code-mixed comments raised macro-F1 from 0.78 to 0.81 (95% CI +0.01 to +0.05; five seeds), and sarcasm caused 31% of the remaining errors. We release the dataset and code.”

Why it changed. Every claim in the revision is specific and supported by a number from the results; the inflated words (“novel”, “state of the art”) are gone.

Stage 9 · chapter 11

The defence

Draft

Planned answer to “Is +3 F1 meaningful?”: “Yes, it is significant.”

Supervisor’s comment

“They will ask what significant means. Give the size, the uncertainty and what it means for a user.”

Revision

“It is 3 points of macro-F1, consistent over all five seeds; the 95% interval on the test set is 1 to 5 points. Accuracy rose from 0.81 to 0.84, so about three more comments in every hundred are classified correctly — mostly negative comments in Latin script, the class that matters most for moderation.”

Why it changed. A strong viva answer gives the number, its uncertainty and its practical meaning, and admits the limits (one platform, one test set).
