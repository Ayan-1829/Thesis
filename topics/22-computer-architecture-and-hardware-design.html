Before we start

Why this matters

Doubling the cache made one program 10× faster and another not faster at all. Same chip, same cache. Explaining why is a thesis.

Architecture research asks how hardware should be organised for the programs people actually run. You can do serious work with a simulator on a laptop or a low-cost FPGA board.

It also shows how to design a rigorous experiment outside the familiar “model and dataset” format: one variable, everything else held fixed, several workloads.

Section 1 of 5

What architecture and hardware research studies

Computer architecture studies how processors, memory and accelerators are organised so that programs run fast and use little energy. Digital hardware design studies the logic circuits they are made of — arithmetic units, controllers, memories — and how they are described in a hardware language, verified and synthesised. Research trades off performance, energy, area, cost and correctness, and most of it is evaluated by simulation or on an FPGA — no chip factory needed.

How it differs from IoT and networks (chapter 19): those study how devices communicate; this field studies what is inside the processor and the circuits themselves.

Section 2 of 5

How architecture research is done

Simulation

  • gem5 (full system), ChampSim (caches and prefetchers), small trace-driven simulators
  • explore many designs cheaply and repeatably
  • slow, and only as accurate as the model

Hardware

  • FPGA prototypes in Verilog or VHDL, or high-level synthesis from C
  • real performance counters on your own CPU (perf)
  • convincing numbers for area, frequency and energy; fewer designs tried
A strong undergraduate thesis often simulates many configurations, then builds or measures the best one on real hardware.

Section 2 of 5

The digital hardware design flow

  1. Specifyinputs, outputs, timing and the target: FPGA or ASIC
  2. Design (RTL)describe the circuit in Verilog or VHDL, or in C with HLS
  3. Verifytestbenches, a reference model, coverage, assertions
  4. Synthesisemap to gates or FPGA cells; area and power reports
  5. Timingstatic timing analysis gives the maximum clock frequency
  6. Prototype and measurerun on the board; measure speed, power, correctness
  • Each step produces evidence for the thesis: the design, the verification results, the area / timing / power reports and the measurements.
  • Verification usually takes more time than design: plan for it.
  • Compare with a baseline circuit (e.g. an exact multiplier, a vendor IP core) synthesised with the same tool, device and settings.
  • State the tool versions, the device and the constraints: synthesis results depend on all three.

Section 2 of 5

Designing a fair simulation experiment

  1. QuestionHow does L1 data-cache size affect miss rate?
  2. Baselinea stated configuration, e.g. 32 KB, 2-way, 64-byte blocks, LRU
  3. One variablechange only the size: 1 to 64 KB
  4. Held fixedblock size, associativity, replacement, L2, core model
  5. Workloadsseveral, with different access patterns; warm the cache up first
  • Put the full configuration in a table: an examiner must be able to rerun it.
  • Use standard benchmark suites, not one program you wrote.
  • Report results per workload as well as the average: the interesting finding is usually why workloads differ.
  • For speed-ups across benchmarks, use the geometric mean.

Evaluation

MetricMeaning
Execution time, speed-upthe measure that matters to users
IPC / CPIinstructions per cycle, or cycles per instruction
Miss rate, MPKIcache misses per access, or per thousand instructions
Energy, powerjoules per task; watts
AreaLUTs, flip-flops, DSPs on an FPGA; mm² on a chip
Maximum clock frequencyfrom timing analysis
  • Report energy per task, not only power: a faster design may use more power but less energy.
  • Miss rate alone can mislead: a bigger cache may be slower to access.
  • Simulators are deterministic, but workloads and inputs vary: use several and report each.

Thesis ideas

StarterAn approximate multiplier

Design exact and approximate 8-bit multipliers in Verilog; compare area, delay and power on an FPGA, and image quality when used in a blur filter.

StarterCache size and workloads

Sweep cache size, block size and associativity in a simulator for three benchmark kernels; explain each curve with the three Cs.

StarterBranch predictors compared

Implement 2-bit, gshare and a perceptron predictor in ChampSim; compare accuracy on embedded benchmarks.

StandardA tiny CNN accelerator on an FPGA

Build a convolution engine with HLS; compare images per second and per joule with a CPU.

StandardA custom RISC-V instruction

Add one instruction to an open core; measure speed-up against area and frequency.

AmbitiousA prefetcher for image kernels

Design a prefetcher in ChampSim or gem5 and evaluate it against standard prefetchers.

  3. Week 3read 5 recent papers (IEEE CAL is short and readable)

Pitfalls: changing two parameters at once, one workload only, configuration not stated, no warm-up, power reported without energy, simulated numbers presented as if measured on silicon.

Worked example 1 of 2

Worked example 1

A simulator gives these L1 miss rates (2-way, 64-byte blocks) for cache sizes 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64 KB. Matrix multiply: 42.5, 38.6, 36.6, 4.8, 2.9, 2.9, 2.9%. Streaming sum: 12.5% at every size. Linked list: 49.5, 48.7, 47.1, 43.6, 35.3, 6.3, 6.3%. Explain the results.
Show the solution
Each workload has a different working set. Matrix multiply re-reads matrix B (8 KB) for every row of A: once B fits, at 8 KB, capacity misses almost disappear. The stream reads 512 KB once per pass, which never fits, so every block is a compulsory or capacity miss: one miss per 64-byte block of 8-byte values = 1 ÷ 8 = 12.5%, whatever the size. The list’s nodes occupy 32 KB in random order, so it improves slowly and then sharply when they all fit; 6.3% is left over from the first pass. Conclusion for the thesis: “a larger L1 helps only when the working set fits; streaming code needs prefetching instead” — and report each workload, because the average hides all of this.

Worked example 2 of 2

Worked example 2

Your FPGA accelerator makes the convolution part of a program 10× faster. Convolution was 40% of the run time. What is the overall speed-up?
Show the solution
Amdahl’s law: new time = 0.60 + 0.40 ÷ 10 = 0.64 of the old time, so speed-up = 1 ÷ 0.64 ≈ 1.56×. Even an infinitely fast accelerator gives at most 1 ÷ 0.60 ≈ 1.67×. Report the end-to-end speed-up, not only the kernel’s.

Practice

Practice (1 of 2)

Try each one yourself first, then check the answer.

1. Name the three Cs of cache misses.
Show the answer
Compulsory (first access to a block), capacity (the cache is too small for the working set) and conflict (blocks map to the same set).
2. Simulation or FPGA: one advantage of each.
Show the answer
Simulation: many configurations quickly and repeatably. FPGA: real area, frequency and energy numbers.
3. In a cache-size experiment, what must be held fixed?
Show the answer
Block size, associativity, replacement policy, the rest of the memory hierarchy, the core model, the workloads and their inputs — everything except the size.

Practice

Practice (2 of 2)

Try each one yourself first, then check the answer.

4. Why report energy per task and not only power?
Show the answer
A faster design can draw more power yet finish sooner and use less energy overall.
5. List the steps of the digital hardware design flow.
Show the answer
Specify, design at register-transfer level (Verilog/VHDL or HLS), verify with testbenches, synthesise, run timing analysis, then prototype and measure on the board.
6. Why use the geometric mean for speed-ups?
Show the answer
Speed-ups are ratios; the geometric mean treats a 2× gain and a 2× loss symmetrically and does not depend on which design is the reference.

Summary

Summary

  • Architecture and hardware research balances performance, energy, area, cost and correctness; it studies what is inside the processor, not how devices communicate.
  • Hardware work follows a design flow: specify, design (RTL), verify, synthesise, analyse timing, prototype and measure.
  • Most undergraduate work uses simulators (gem5, ChampSim) or FPGA boards.
  • Design experiments like a scientist: a stated baseline, one variable changed, everything else fixed, several workloads.
  • Report per-workload results, energy per task and the full configuration; use geometric means for speed-ups.
  • Explain results with models: the three Cs, Amdahl’s law, the roofline.
  • Venues: ISCA, MICRO, HPCA, ASPLOS, FPGA, FCCM, DAC.
