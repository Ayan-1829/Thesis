Before we start

Why this matters

You read a 12-page paper from start to end, carefully, for three hours. A week later you remember almost nothing.

Researchers do not read papers like novels. They read in passes, with questions, and they write notes they can use later. That is how they cover dozens of papers without drowning.

This chapter gives you a reading method, a note template and a literature matrix — the raw material of your literature review.

Reading strategy

The three-pass method

Reading strategy

The three-pass method (cont.)

  • Pass 1 — 5 to 10 minutes. Title, abstract, introduction, section headings, figures, conclusion. Decide: relevant? read on?
  • Pass 2 — about an hour. Read everything except the hardest proofs and details. Study figures and tables. Mark references to read. Summarise it in your own words.
  • Pass 3 — several hours. Only for the papers your thesis builds on. Re-derive, or re-implement; question every assumption; note strengths, weaknesses and ideas.

After S. Keshav, “How to Read a Paper”, ACM SIGCOMM Computer Communication Review, 2007.

Reading strategy

Questions to ask while reading

Problem

What exactly is the problem, and why does it matter?

Novelty

What is new compared with earlier work? Is the claim supported?

Method

What did they do? Could I explain it to a classmate in two minutes?

Evidence

Which data, baselines and metrics? Do the numbers support the conclusion? Any statistics?

Limits

What assumptions? Where would it fail? What did they not test?

For me

What can I reuse (dataset, baseline, metric, code)? What would I do differently?

Reading results tables critically

ModelAccuracyF1
Baseline (2019)81.20.62
Strong baseline (2023)86.90.74
Proposed87.40.75
  • Is +0.5 accuracy significant, or within run-to-run noise? Look for standard deviations or tests.
  • Why does accuracy (87%) differ so much from F1 (0.75)? Probably class imbalance.
  • Were all models tuned equally, on the same split?
  • Is the test set large enough? 0.5% of 200 samples is one example.
  • Which classes or cases does the method still get wrong?

  • Write notes in your own words; copied sentences in notes become accidental plagiarism later.
  • Mark exact quotes with quotation marks and the page.
  • Give every paper a tag (theme, method, dataset).
  • Keep notes in one place: Zotero notes, a spreadsheet or Obsidian.

Choose one tool on day one and put every paper in it. Re-finding a paper you read three months ago costs far more than saving it now.

Notes and the literature matrix

Organising your library

  • Folders or tags by theme (e.g. datasets, transformer models, augmentation, evaluation).
  • A tag for reading status: to read, pass 1, pass 2, key paper.
  • Consistent citation keys (author + year + first word: sharma2023bangla).
  • One shared group library if you work in a pair.

A weekly reading rhythm

Monday: pass 1 on 10 new titles · Wednesday: pass 2 on the best 2–3 · Friday: update the matrix and write one paragraph comparing what you read.

Part 9 of 9

Understanding hard papers

  • Read a survey or tutorial on the topic first; then the paper makes sense.
  • Look up unknown terms in a textbook, not only in a chatbot — and check the answer.
  • Find the paper’s code and run it; code removes ambiguity.
  • Find a talk or blog by the authors: they often explain the idea more simply.
  • Discuss it in a reading group with classmates — explaining is the best test of understanding.
  • It is normal to read a key paper three or four times.

Problem: skin-lesion classification on mobile devices. Method: lightweight CNN with … (fill after pass 2). Data: HAM10000 (≈10,000 dermatoscopic images, 7 classes). Results: +2% balanced accuracy over MobileNetV2. Limitations: (pass 2) single dataset, no external test. Relevance: candidate baseline for my mobile skin-cancer thesis.

Worked example 2 of 2

Worked example 2

A table shows the proposed model at 92.1% accuracy and the baseline at 91.8%, one run each, on 300 test images. What do you conclude?
Show the solution
0.3% of 300 images is about one image. With one run and no variance, the difference is within noise. You cannot conclude the proposed model is better; you would need several runs, variance and a significance test (chapter 8).

Practice (2 of 2)

Try each one yourself first, then check the answer.

4. Why must notes be in your own words?
Show the answer
Copied sentences end up in the thesis without quotation marks and become plagiarism; paraphrasing also tests your understanding.
5. What is a literature matrix?
Show the answer
A table with one row per paper and columns such as problem, data, method, metric, results and limitations; it shows patterns and gaps across papers.
6. Give three ways to understand a paper you find too hard.
Show the answer
Read a survey or tutorial first; run the authors’ code; watch the authors’ talk; discuss in a reading group; reread it several times.

Summary

Summary

  • Read in passes: a 10-minute scan, a one-hour read, and a deep read only for key papers.
  • Ask the same questions of every paper: problem, novelty, method, evidence, limits, use for me.
  • Read results critically: significance, imbalance, fair tuning, test-set size, failure cases.
  • Write one-paper summaries in your own words; mark quotes with quotation marks.
  • Keep a literature matrix: it reveals patterns and the gap.
  • Use one reference manager from day one; tag papers by theme and status.
