Before we start

Why this matters

Two students build the same face-recognition app. One gets a project grade; the other writes a thesis that is cited by others. What is the difference?

The second student asked a question — does the app still work for faces with masks, and why not? — measured it fairly against existing methods, and explained the answer. That is research.

This chapter explains what a thesis is, what it can contribute, how the year usually runs, and how to work with your supervisor so you finish on time.

Part 1 of 13

What a thesis is

A thesis is a written report of your own research: you ask a question nobody has fully answered, collect evidence in a planned way, and explain what the evidence shows. It ends with a contribution — something the field knows or can do now that it could not before.

  • It starts from a question, not from a product idea.
  • It builds on existing work: you must show what is already known.
  • It uses a method others could repeat.
  • It reports evidence honestly, including what did not work.
  • It is judged by examiners on originality, rigour and clarity.
In one sentence: a thesis answers a research question with evidence, and shows why the answer matters.

What research is

Research versus development

Development project

  • Goal: a working product
  • Success: “it works and users like it”
  • Novelty: not required
  • Comparison with others: optional
  • Example: build a Bangla OCR mobile app

Research thesis

  • Goal: new knowledge
  • Success: the question is answered with evidence
  • Novelty: required — something new, or new evidence
  • Comparison with existing work: essential
  • Example: does fine-tuning on synthetic handwriting improve Bangla OCR on old documents?
Most good computer science theses also build something — a model, a tool, a system. The difference is that it is built to answer a question, and it is measured against what already exists.

What a thesis can contribute

A new method

An algorithm, model or technique that beats or simplifies the current best.

A new dataset or benchmark

Data that did not exist, with labels, documentation and baseline results — very valuable for local languages and problems.

An empirical study

A careful comparison of existing methods on a new setting, explaining when each works.

A system with evaluation

A tool or prototype, measured with users or experiments.

Part 4 of 13

What a thesis can contribute (cont.)

A survey or systematic review

A structured map of a field that identifies trends and open problems.

A replication

Repeating a published study on new data to test whether its findings hold.

An undergraduate thesis does not need to change the world: a clear, honest, well-measured contribution in a narrow area is a success.

The research cycle (cont.)

Research is a loop, not a straight line. You will go round it several times: early experiments change your questions, and new papers change your method.

  • Question → what exactly do you want to know?
  • Literature → what is already known?
  • Method → how will you find out?
  • Experiment → collect the evidence.
  • Analysis → what does it show, and how sure are you?
  • Writing → explain it so others can check and build on it.

Your supervisor is a guide, not a manager. You own the thesis: plan the work, propose next steps and bring evidence to every meeting.

The research process

Working with your supervisor

  • Agree a meeting rhythm (weekly or fortnightly) and keep it.
  • Send a short progress report the day before: done, problems, plan, questions.
  • Bring evidence: numbers, plots, draft pages — not just “I am working on it”.
  • Write down the decisions and actions at the end of each meeting and email them back.
  • Ask early when you are stuck for more than a few days.
  • Never disappear: a short “no progress, because…” email is better than silence.

Before each meeting

  1. What did I do since last time?
  2. What did I learn (results)?
  3. What blocked me?
  4. What do I plan next?
  5. Which decision do I need from my supervisor?

Habits of good researchers

Read every week

Two or three papers a week, with notes. Reading is part of the work, not a phase that ends.

Keep a research diary

Date, what you tried, the result, what you will try next. You will need it when writing.

Start small

Run a tiny experiment in week one. A small working pipeline beats a big plan.

Version everything

Code and LaTeX in Git, data with a README, results with the command that produced them.

Write as you go

A paragraph about every experiment, the day you run it. The thesis is then mostly editing.

Back up

Three copies, two places, one off your laptop. A lost laptop must not cost the thesis.

Worked example 1 of 3

Worked example 1

Classify each as development (D) or research (R): (a) “Build a website for our department’s alumni.” (b) “Which of three Bangla sentiment models generalises best to social-media text from a different year?” (c) “Develop a library management app with barcode scanning.” (d) “Does adding traffic-light timing data reduce the error of bus-arrival prediction in Dhaka?”
Show the solution
(a) D — the goal is a working product. (b) R — a comparison question answered with evidence. (c) D — a product. (d) R — a question about an effect, measured against a baseline.

Worked example 2 of 3

Worked example 2

Turn this project into a research question: “An app that detects plant diseases from leaf photos.”
Show the solution
Find what is unknown. For example: “How much does accuracy drop when a model trained on lab photos is used on farmers’ phone photos in the field, and does data augmentation close the gap?” Now there is a question, a comparison (lab vs field, with vs without augmentation) and a measurable answer.

Worked example 3 of 3

Worked example 3

A pair of students has 12 months. Their data collection is delayed by one month. What do they change?
Show the solution
They do not move the final deadline. They (1) start the literature review and method on a public dataset in the meantime, (2) shorten the experiment phase by running fewer variants, (3) write the background chapters during the delay, and (4) tell their supervisor at once and agree the new plan in writing.

Practice

Practice (1 of 2)

Try each one yourself first, then check the answer.

1. Name three things that make a thesis different from a software project.
Show the answer
It answers a research question; it compares with existing work; it reports evidence from a planned method (and is judged on originality and rigour, not only on whether the software works).
2. List four kinds of contribution an undergraduate thesis can make.
Show the answer
A new method, a new dataset or benchmark, an empirical comparison of existing methods in a new setting, a system with evaluation, a survey, or a replication study.
3. Put the steps of the research cycle in order: analysis, question, writing, experiment, literature, method.
Show the answer
Question → literature → method → experiment → analysis → writing, and then back to new questions.

Practice

Practice (2 of 2)

Try each one yourself first, then check the answer.

4. What should a weekly report to your supervisor contain?
Show the answer
What you did, what you learned (results, with numbers or plots), what blocked you, the plan for next week and the decisions you need.
5. Your experiments show your idea does not beat the baseline. Is the thesis failed?
Show the answer
No. A negative result that is measured fairly and explained (why it did not work, in which cases) is a valid contribution. Report it honestly and analyse it.
6. Why should you build a simple baseline in the first month?
Show the answer
It tests the data and the pipeline early, gives a number to beat, reveals problems while there is still time, and gives you something concrete to discuss with your supervisor.

Summary

Summary

  • A thesis answers a research question with evidence and states its contribution.
  • Research differs from development: it needs a question, a method, a comparison with existing work and honest evidence.
  • Contributions include new methods, datasets, empirical studies, evaluated systems, surveys and replications.
  • Research is a cycle: question, literature, method, experiment, analysis, writing — repeated.
  • You own the thesis; meet your supervisor regularly with evidence and a plan.
  • Start small, read every week, write as you go, version and back up everything.
