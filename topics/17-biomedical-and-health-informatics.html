Before we start

Why this matters

A test is 95% sensitive and 95% specific, and it says you have a rare disease. Should you panic? Surprisingly, you are probably fine.

When a disease affects 1 person in 100, most positive results from that test are wrong. Health research rewards students who understand both the computing and the clinical meaning of their numbers — and who treat patient data with care.

This chapter covers problems, methods, data, the right metrics, ethics and venues in biomedical informatics.

Part 1 of 12

What biomedical informatics research studies

Biomedical and health informatics applies computing to medicine and biology: analysing medical images and signals, predicting disease risk from records, processing clinical text, analysing genes and proteins, and building digital-health tools. Errors can harm patients, so evaluation and ethics matter even more than in other fields.

Part 3 of 12

Working with clinical problems

  • Find a clinical collaborator (doctor, public-health expert): they define what matters and check plausibility.
  • Decide the clinical use: screening, diagnosis support, triage, prognosis? It decides the metric and threshold.
  • Use patient-level splits and, ideally, an external test set from another hospital.
  • Report sensitivity and specificity at a clinically chosen threshold, and calibration.
Patient data needs ethics approval, de-identification and a data-use agreement. Never put identifiable patient data on a public cloud or into a public AI tool.

Methods

Key methods to know

Image models

CNNs and U-Net for classification and segmentation of X-ray, CT, MRI, fundus and histopathology images.

Signal processing

Filtering, R-peak detection, spectral features; 1-D CNNs for ECG and EEG.

Tabular prediction

Logistic regression and gradient boosting with calibration; survival analysis (Cox models).

Clinical NLP

Named-entity recognition for symptoms, drugs and diseases; domain-specific language models.

Bioinformatics

Sequence alignment, gene-expression analysis, feature selection, protein structure tools.

Interpretability

Saliency maps, SHAP and simple models clinicians can check.

Thesis ideas

StarterExternal validation of a published model

Test a public chest X-ray or skin-lesion model on another dataset; measure the drop and subgroup performance.

StandardWearable ECG arrhythmia detection

Single-lead ECG classification on PhysioNet data; test on noisy recordings.

StandardCalibrated risk prediction

Predict severity of a local disease from admission data with a doctor; report calibration and decision thresholds.

StandardBangla health-message NLP

Extract symptoms from anonymised telemedicine messages.

AmbitiousFederated learning across hospitals

Simulate training across sites without sharing data; compare with central training.

Thesis ideas

Getting started in four weeks

  1. Week 1basics: sensitivity, specificity, PPV, ROC, calibration
  2. Week 2get access to a public dataset (PhysioNet training if needed)
  3. Week 3read 5 papers via PubMed and MICCAI; meet a clinician
  4. Week 4baseline model with patient-level split; one-page idea
Pitfalls: image-level instead of patient-level splits, accuracy on rare diseases, no external test, models learning hospital or scanner artefacts, identifiable data on public tools.

Worked example 1 of 2

Worked example 1

A test has 95% sensitivity and 95% specificity; prevalence is 1%. Out of 10,000 people, what is the PPV?
Show the solution
Sick: 100; detected: 95. Healthy: 9,900; false positives: 5% = 495. PPV = 95 ÷ (95 + 495) ≈ 16%. Only one in six positives is truly sick — so this test is for screening followed by confirmation, not diagnosis.

Worked example 2 of 2

Worked example 2

A pneumonia model trained in hospital A has AUC 0.93 internally but 0.78 at hospital B. What could cause it, and what do you do?
Show the solution
Differences in scanners, patient populations, labelling practices or shortcut features (e.g. text markers on images). Report both results honestly, analyse errors, try normalisation, augmentation or fine-tuning with a small local sample, and discuss limitations.

Practice

Practice (1 of 2)

Try each one yourself first, then check the answer.

1. Define sensitivity and specificity.
Show the answer
Sensitivity: share of truly sick patients the test detects. Specificity: share of truly healthy patients the test clears.
2. Why does PPV depend on prevalence?
Show the answer
With a rare disease, even a small false-positive rate among the many healthy people produces more false than true positives.
3. Why split by patient?
Show the answer
Several images or records from one patient in train and test let the model recognise the patient, inflating results.

Practice

Practice (2 of 2)

Try each one yourself first, then check the answer.

4. What approvals does patient data need?
Show the answer
Ethics approval, de-identification, and a data-use agreement or credentialed access (e.g. PhysioNet for MIMIC).
5. Name two medical-imaging venues.
Show the answer
MICCAI, ISBI (journals: IEEE TMI, Medical Image Analysis).
6. What is calibration and why does it matter clinically?
Show the answer
Whether predicted probabilities match observed frequencies; doctors act on risk estimates, so a 30% prediction must mean about 30% risk.

Summary

Summary

  • Biomedical informatics covers imaging, biosignals, clinical prediction, clinical text, bioinformatics and digital health.
  • Work with a clinician; define the clinical use, which decides the metric and threshold.
  • Split by patient, validate externally, report sensitivity, specificity, PPV/NPV, calibration and confidence intervals.
  • Prevalence changes PPV dramatically: understand it before claiming a test is useful.
  • Patient data needs approval, de-identification and agreements; never use public AI tools on it.
  • Venues: MICCAI, ISBI, AMIA, JAMIA, ML4H, CHIL, Computing in Cardiology.
