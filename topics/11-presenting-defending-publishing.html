Before we start

Why this matters

One year of work. Fifteen minutes of slides. Then a professor leans forward and says, “Just one small question…”

A defence is not a quiz on facts; it is a conversation in which you show that you understand your work, its strengths and its limits. Good slides and honest answers make it easy.

This chapter prepares you for the presentation, the viva and turning your thesis into a published paper.

Presenting

Structure of a defence talk

  1. Hookthe problem in one slide, with an example
  2. Gapwhy existing work is not enough
  3. Questionsyour RQs and contributions
  4. Methodone diagram
  5. Resultsthe main table or plot, then the key insight
  6. Analysisablation, errors, limitations
  7. Conclusionanswers to the RQs; future work

Timing for 15 minutes

Problem and gap 3 · method 3 · results 5 · analysis and limitations 2 · conclusion 1 · buffer 1. About one slide per minute.

Presenting

Rehearsing

  • Rehearse aloud with a timer at least three times; cut until you finish with a minute to spare.
  • Present to classmates who do not know your topic, and ask what was unclear.
  • Record yourself once: watch for filler words and turning your back to the audience.
  • Prepare the laptop, adapter, a PDF copy on a USB drive and in the cloud.
  • Know your first two sentences by heart; the rest follows.

Part 5 of 10

The viva: questions to expect

The defence

Answering questions

  1. Listen to the whole question; pause; thank the examiner if it helps you think.
  2. Repeat or rephrase it if unsure: “Do you mean…?”
  3. Answer directly first, then give the evidence (a backup slide helps).
  4. If you do not know, say so honestly, then say how you would find out.
  5. Agree with fair criticism and explain the limitation; do not become defensive.
Examiners test whether you understand your work. An honest “we did not test that; I expect … because …” scores better than a confident guess.

Part 7 of 10

Posters

  • A0 or A1, portrait or as required; readable from 2 metres.
  • Title that states the finding, not only the topic.
  • Sections: problem · method (diagram) · results (big figure) · conclusion · QR code to the paper or code.
  • About 300–500 words in total.
  • Prepare a 1-minute and a 3-minute explanation.

Poster layout

Three columns read left to right: why (problem, gap) → how (method) → what (results, conclusion). The key figure is the largest element.

Posters and publishing

Publishing your thesis work

Conference

  • 8–10 page paper, peer-reviewed in 2–4 months
  • presented in person or online
  • the main venue type in ML, AI, CV, NLP
  • deadlines fixed; plan backwards

Journal

  • longer article, several review rounds
  • months to a year
  • more weight in many fields and for promotions
  • check quartile and that it is not predatory

Good first venues: workshops at major conferences, regional IEEE conferences with proper peer review, and student research competitions. Your supervisor knows which are respected.

Posters and publishing

Peer review

  • Desk reject: out of scope or badly formatted — check the call for papers.
  • Reviews: usually 2–4 experts, often anonymous.
  • Decision: accept, minor revision, major revision or reject.
  • Rebuttal / response letter: answer every point politely, say what changed and where.
  • A rejection with good reviews is useful: improve and submit elsewhere.

Posters and publishing

Releasing code and data

  • Clean the repository: README, licence, requirements, a script that reproduces the main table.
  • Archive a version with a DOI (Zenodo) and cite it in the thesis.
  • Release models on Hugging Face if allowed.
  • Remove personal data and secrets (API keys!) before publishing.
  • Post a preprint on arXiv if the venue allows it, to share the work early.

Worked example 1 of 3

Worked example 1

An examiner asks: “Why did you not compare with GPT-4?” How do you answer?
Show the solution
Directly and honestly: “We focused on models that can run locally and be fine-tuned on our data, because the target users cannot send private messages to an external API. We did not evaluate GPT-4; based on [ref], we expect it to be strong on standard Bangla but it would need to be tested on code-mixed text. That is a good direction for future work.”

Worked example 2

Turn the slide title “Results” into a message title.
Show the solution
“Continued pre-training improves macro-F1 by 3 points on code-mixed comments” — the audience knows what to look for before they read the table.

Worked example 3 of 3

Worked example 3

A reviewer writes: “The baselines are weak; the comparison with recent transformer models is missing.” Draft your response.
Show the solution
“We thank the reviewer. We have added XLM-R and BanglaBERT as baselines, tuned with the same budget as our model (Table 3, Section 5.1). Our method still improves macro-F1 by 2.4 points over the strongest baseline (p = 0.02).”

Show the answer
Proposal defence, progress presentation, final defence and poster session.

About: problem and gap 3 min, method 3, results 5, analysis and limitations 2, conclusion 1, buffer 1.

Show the answer

Practice (2 of 2)

Try each one yourself first, then check the answer.

4. What makes a good poster?
Show the answer
A finding in the title, one clear message, large figures, little text (300–500 words), a logical why–how–what flow, readable from 2 metres, a QR code.
5. Conference or journal: give two differences.
Show the answer
Conferences: shorter papers, fixed deadlines, faster review, presented live, dominant in ML/AI. Journals: longer, several review rounds, slower, more weight in many fields.
6. What should be removed before releasing code publicly?
Show the answer
Personal data, credentials and API keys, large private datasets you may not share; and add a README and licence.

Summary

Summary

  • Proposal, progress, final defence and poster each have a different goal and focus.
  • Structure the talk: hook, gap, questions, method, results, analysis, conclusion — about one slide per minute.
  • One message per slide, big figures, message titles; rehearse with a timer.
  • In the viva, answer directly, use evidence, admit what you do not know, accept fair criticism.
  • Publish at a suitable conference or journal; respond to reviews point by point.
  • Release clean, documented code and data with a DOI; remove personal data and secrets.
