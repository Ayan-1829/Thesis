Before we start

Why this matters

A thesis is submitted with excellent results. A year later someone finds the test set was used for tuning, and two paragraphs were copied from a blog.

Ethics is not a form to sign at the end; it shapes how you collect data, run experiments, use AI tools and write. Most problems are not deliberate cheating but shortcuts and carelessness.

This chapter lists the rules that protect you, your participants and the trust in your results.

Part 1 of 9

Research integrity

Research is useful only if others can trust it. Integrity means reporting what you really did and found, and giving credit for every idea and word that is not yours.

MisconductMeaning
Fabricationinventing data or results
Falsificationchanging data, removing inconvenient runs, editing images, picking only good seeds
Plagiarismpresenting others’ words, ideas, code or figures as your own
Misconduct can lead to a failed thesis, a cancelled degree, or a retracted paper years later. No result is worth it.

Plagiarism and citing

Quote, paraphrase, summarise

Original

“Transformer models pretrained on large multilingual corpora transfer poorly to code-mixed text because their tokenisers fragment romanised words into many subword pieces.”

Acceptable paraphrase

Multilingual pretrained models struggle with code-mixed text, partly because romanised words are split into many small subword units by their tokenisers [12].

  • Quote rarely, with quotation marks and the citation — only when the exact words matter.
  • Paraphrase: the same idea, your structure and words, with the citation.
  • Summarise: the main point of a longer passage, with the citation.

  • Follow your department’s policy and agree the rules with your supervisor.
  • Keep a short log of what you used and for what.
  • Declare it in the thesis (a short statement in the methodology or acknowledgements).
  • You are responsible for every word and number, whoever helped.

Part 6 of 9

Research with people

  • Informed consent: participants know the purpose, what they do, risks, and that they can stop at any time.
  • Voluntary: no pressure — be careful with your own classmates or students.
  • Privacy: collect only what you need; anonymise; store securely; delete when done.
  • Approval: ask whether your university needs ethics-committee approval for surveys, interviews, user studies or health data.
  • Vulnerable groups (children, patients) need extra protection and usually formal approval.

A consent form contains

  • who you are and your supervisor
  • the purpose of the study
  • what the participant will do, and how long it takes
  • risks and benefits
  • how data is stored, used and anonymised
  • the right to withdraw
  • contact details

Part 8 of 9

Fairness, bias and harm

  • Does the data represent the people the system affects (gender, region, language, skin tone, age)?
  • Report results per group where it matters, not only an average.
  • Could the system be misused (surveillance, deepfakes, attacks)? Say so, and how you limit it.
  • In security research, follow responsible disclosure: tell the vendor before publishing a vulnerability.
  • State the limitations so nobody uses the work beyond what it supports.

Worked example 1 of 3

Worked example 1

Your model gives F1 0.70, 0.71, 0.84, 0.69 and 0.70 on five seeds. You are tempted to report only 0.84. Why not, and what do you report?
Show the solution
Reporting only the best seed is falsification by selection: it misrepresents the expected performance. Report the mean ± sd (0.728 ± 0.063), mention the unusual run, and investigate why that run differs.

Worked example 2 of 3

Worked example 2

You plan a survey of 200 students about how they use AI for assignments. List the ethical steps.
Show the solution
Check whether ethics approval is needed; write a consent statement (purpose, voluntary, anonymous, right to stop); collect no names or IDs; store responses securely; avoid pressure (do not survey your own juniors as their tutor); report only aggregated results; delete raw data after the thesis as stated.

Worked example 3 of 3

Worked example 3

You found a SQL-injection vulnerability in a local e-commerce site during your security thesis. What should you do?
Show the solution
Do not exploit it further or publish it immediately. Inform your supervisor, report it privately to the company (responsible disclosure) with enough detail to fix it, give them time, and describe it in the thesis without enabling attacks — ideally after it is fixed.

Practice

Practice (1 of 2)

Try each one yourself first, then check the answer.

1. Define fabrication, falsification and plagiarism.
Show the answer
Fabrication: inventing data or results. Falsification: manipulating or selectively reporting data or results. Plagiarism: presenting others’ words, ideas, code or figures as your own.
2. Is changing a few words of a sentence and citing it acceptable?
Show the answer
No — that is patchwriting. Understand the idea, write it in your own structure and words, and cite it; or quote it exactly with quotation marks.
3. Give two acceptable and two unacceptable uses of AI tools.
Show the answer
Acceptable (if allowed): brainstorming search terms; grammar suggestions on your own text. Unacceptable: submitting generated text as yours; citing unverified AI-produced references; uploading personal data.

Practice

Practice (2 of 2)

Try each one yourself first, then check the answer.

4. What does informed consent require?
Show the answer
Participants understand the purpose, what they will do, risks, how data is used and stored, and that participation is voluntary and they can withdraw; they agree before taking part.
5. What must you check before using a public dataset?
Show the answer
Its licence and terms (e.g. research only, attribution), any personal data it contains, and the paper to cite.
6. What is responsible disclosure?
Show the answer
Reporting a security vulnerability privately to the affected vendor and allowing time to fix it before publishing details.

Summary

Summary

  • Never fabricate, falsify or plagiarise; report all runs and every source.
  • Paraphrase in your own words and cite; quote rarely, with quotation marks.
  • Use AI tools only as your department allows; verify everything; declare how you used them.
  • Research with people needs informed consent, privacy and often ethics approval.
  • Check dataset, scraping and code licences; protect personal and medical data.
  • Consider bias, misuse and responsible disclosure; agree authorship and acknowledge help.
