Before we start

Why this matters

Week 30. The method still loses to the baseline, your supervisor is at a conference, your group partner is quiet, and the deadline has not moved. Every thesis has a week like this.

The research chapters teach what to do. This one is about getting it done: planning time, working with a supervisor and a group, and handling the experiments that fail.

None of it is a sign that you are doing badly. It is what research is like — and there are good ways through it.

Section 1 of 4

Why theses run late

  • People underestimate how long their own tasks take, even when they know past tasks ran late — the planning fallacy.
  • Data collection, cleaning and debugging take two to three times longer than planned.
  • The last 10% (writing, figures, formatting, references) takes far more than 10% of the time.
  • Waiting — for data access, ethics approval, supervisor feedback — is invisible in most plans.
Plan for it: add a 20–30% buffer to every phase, and start the tasks that involve waiting for other people first.

Section 1 of 4

Plan backwards from the deadline

  1. Fix the datessubmission, defence, department deadlines
  2. Work backfinal draft → full draft → results → experiments → data
  3. Add buffers2–3 weeks before submission
  4. Weekly goalsone concrete output per week
  5. Review monthlywith your supervisor; re-plan honestly

Define a minimum thesis

Write down the smallest result that still answers your research question — e.g. one baseline, your method and one ablation on one dataset. Finish that first; everything else is a bonus. Use the planner in chapter 1 and the milestones.

Section 1 of 4

A working week

Protected blocks

Two or three blocks of 2–3 hours a week for thesis work only, with the phone away.

Write every week

Even half a page: method notes, a results paragraph. Writing reveals gaps early.

A weekly log

Three lines: done, blocked, next. It becomes your meeting agenda.

Small first steps

When stuck, choose a 20-minute task: plot one result, read one section.

Section 2 of 4

Meetings that work

  • Send a short agenda a day before: what you did, results (with a figure), questions, what you plan next.
  • Bring decisions to make, not only updates: “Should I add dataset B or do error analysis?”
  • Write the agreed actions at the end and email them the same day.
  • Keep a meeting log: date, decisions, actions, deadlines.

An agenda email

Subject: Thesis meeting Tuesday — agenda
1. Done: baseline F1 0.68 ± 0.01 (figure attached).
2. Problem: BanglaBERT runs out of GPU memory at batch size 32.
3. Decision needed: smaller batch, or a smaller model?
4. Next week: ablation without augmentation.

Section 2 of 4

When the supervision relationship is hard

SituationWhat usually helps
No reply for weeksone short email with a single question and a proposed time; then ask in person after class or office hours
Conflicting advice from two supervisorswrite both options down and ask them to decide together, in one meeting or one email thread
You disagree with the directionbring evidence (a pilot result, two papers) and propose an alternative; accept the decision or ask why
Feedback feels harshseparate the content from the tone; ask “what would a good version look like?”
Real problems persisttalk to the thesis coordinator or head of department early, calmly and with your meeting log
Most supervisors supervise many students. Clear, short, prepared communication is the single most effective thing you control.

Section 2 of 4

Thesis in a group

  • Many departments run theses in groups of two or three: agree who does what in writing at the start.
  • One shared Git repository, one shared reference library, one shared document.
  • Meet as a group weekly; rotate who writes the agenda.
  • If a member stops contributing, raise it with them first, then with the supervisor — early, not at the defence.
  • In the thesis, state each member’s contribution if your department asks for it.

Section 3 of 4

When experiments fail

  1. Can the model overfit 20 examples? If not, the pipeline has a bug.
  2. Do you reproduce a known result (a baseline from a paper) with your code?
  3. Look at the data: labels, shapes, a few raw examples after every step.
  4. Change one thing at a time and log it.
  5. Ask: is the task actually learnable from this data?
Debugging is part of research. Record what you tried — failed attempts often become a useful paragraph in the methodology or the limitations.

Section 3 of 4

Negative results are results

Your method loses to the baseline. The thesis has not failed: your research question asked whether it would help, and the answer is “no” — now explain why.

  • Analyse where it loses: classes, data sizes, input lengths.
  • Find conditions where it helps, if any (small data? noisy labels?).
  • Check it was a fair fight: same tuning budget, no bugs, strong baseline.
  • Report it honestly; the examiners will judge the quality of the investigation.

Writing it up

“Contrary to our hypothesis, continued pre-training did not improve macro-F1 (−0.4 points, 95% CI −1.3 to 0.5). Error analysis suggests the unlabelled corpus was too formal: gains appeared only on comments written in Bangla script.”

Negative results are welcomed by workshops such as “I Can’t Believe It’s Not Better!”.

Section 3 of 4

Persist or change direction?

SignalUsually means
Data you need will not arrive in timechange the data or the question now — waiting rarely helps
The method works, but less than hopedpersist: analyse, ablate, report
A paper appears that did your ideanot the end: different data, a local setting, a deeper analysis or a replication are still contributions
Nothing learnable after the sanity checksdiscuss a pivot with your supervisor; keep what you built
Time already spent is not a reason to continue (the sunk-cost trap). Decide on what the remaining months can still achieve.

Section 4 of 4

Looking after yourself

  • A thesis is long: sleep, exercise and time with people keep your thinking sharp.
  • Talk to peers in the same stage; a weekly writing group helps many students.
  • Stuck and anxious for weeks is common — and a reason to talk to your supervisor early, not to hide.
  • Your university’s student counselling service is there for exactly this.
  • The thesis is one piece of work. It does not measure your worth.

Worked example 1 of 2

Worked example 1

Six weeks to submission. Left: two experiments (one week each), error analysis (one week), and writing the methodology, results and discussion chapters (about three weeks). What do you do?
Show the solution
Seven weeks of work in six, with no buffer. Agree a minimum thesis with your supervisor: keep the experiment that answers the main research question, drop or shorten the other (mention it as future work). Write the methodology now, while the experiment runs. Do a smaller error analysis (50 errors). Plan: weeks 1–2 experiment + methodology; week 3 error analysis + results; weeks 4–5 discussion, introduction, abstract; week 6 buffer and proofreading.

Worked example 2 of 2

Worked example 2

Your method scores 0.776 ± 0.010 macro-F1; the baseline 0.791 ± 0.008 (5 seeds). How do you present this in the results and discussion?
Show the solution
Report it directly: the method is 1.5 points worse, consistently over seeds. Then analyse: per-class results, performance by input length or domain, and whether any subset benefits. Check fairness: the same tuning budget, a bug-free pipeline (both reproduce published numbers?). Discussion: answer the research question (“No — under these conditions it does not help”), give the most likely reasons with evidence, and suggest what would test them. This is a complete, honest thesis.

Practice

Practice (1 of 2)

Try each one yourself first, then check the answer.

1. What is the planning fallacy, and how do you plan around it?
Show the answer
The tendency to underestimate how long your own tasks will take. Plan backwards from the deadline, add 20–30% buffers, start tasks that depend on others first, and re-plan monthly.
2. What is a minimum thesis?
Show the answer
The smallest result that still answers the research question, finished first so the rest is a bonus.
3. What should an agenda for a supervisor meeting contain?
Show the answer
What you did, results (ideally a figure), problems, the decisions you need, and the plan for next week.

Practice (2 of 2)

Try each one yourself first, then check the answer.

4. Your model does not learn. Give three checks.
Show the answer
Overfit a tiny subset; reproduce a known baseline result; inspect the data and labels after each step; change one thing at a time.
5. Why is a well-analysed negative result still a thesis?
Show the answer
It answers the research question with evidence and explains why; the examiners judge the quality of the investigation, not whether the hypothesis was right.
6. A paper with your idea appears in month 5. What can you still contribute?
Show the answer
Different or local data, a different setting or language, a deeper analysis, a replication, or a comparison with that paper.

Summary

Summary

  • Theses run late for predictable reasons: plan backwards, add buffers, start tasks that need other people first.
  • Define a minimum thesis and finish it first; write every week.
  • Prepare meetings: an agenda, results, decisions needed; record actions in a meeting log.
  • Handle supervision and group problems early, calmly and in writing.
  • Debug failed experiments systematically; a well-analysed negative result is still a thesis.
  • Look after yourself and use the people and services around you.
