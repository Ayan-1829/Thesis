Choosing a Topic and Research Questions

Where topics come from, narrowing an area to a problem and a research question, the FINER check, hypotheses and objectives, scoping the work, and getting the topic agreed.

StartAll chapters

Before we start

Why this matters

“Sir, I want to do something with AI.” The supervisor smiles; it is the fifth time this week. It is an interest, not a thesis.

A thesis needs a question narrow enough to answer in a year, with data you can get and a result you can measure. The topic decides more about your thesis than anything you do later.

This chapter turns an interest into a precise research question and shows how to test it before you commit.

Part 1 of 11

Where good topics come from

Your interests

A course you enjoyed, a project you are proud of, a problem you meet every day. You will live with the topic for a year.

Recent papers

The “future work” and “limitations” sections of recent papers list open problems, often stated by the authors themselves.

Local problems

Bangla language, local agriculture, Dhaka traffic, local health data: problems the big labs ignore, where your data and context are an advantage.

Your supervisor

Supervisors often have ideas, data or ongoing projects. A topic inside their expertise gets better guidance.

Part 1 of 11

Where good topics come from (cont.)

Industry and society

A company or NGO problem with real data and real users can become a strong thesis — if you can publish the results.

Replication

“Does this published result hold on our data?” is a modest but honest and useful starting point.

From area to objectives (cont.)

A topic becomes researchable only when it is narrow enough to answer. Each step narrows the one before:

Finding a topic

A good topic is…

CriterionAsk yourself
InterestingWill I still care about it in ten months?
FeasibleCan I finish it with my skills, time and hardware (or a free GPU)?
Data-readyDoes the data exist, or can I collect and label it in a few weeks?
NovelIs there something not yet done — a new setting, data, method or comparison?
SupervisedDoes a supervisor know the area?
MeasurableIs there a clear way to tell whether I succeeded?
UsefulWho benefits if I answer it?

Part 5 of 11

Research questions

A research question (RQ) states exactly what you want to find out. Most theses have one main question and two or three sub-questions.

  • It is a question, not a task (“Does…?”, “How…?”, “Which…?”, “To what extent…?”).
  • It names the population or data, the intervention or method, the comparison and the outcome.
  • It can be answered with evidence within your time.
  • Its answer is not obvious in advance.

Templates

  • Does X improve Y for Z compared with W?
  • Which of A, B, C works best for task on data?
  • How can we design a system that does T under constraint C?
  • Why does method M fail on case K?

Writing research questions

The FINER check

LetterMeaningFails when…
FFeasible: time, data, skills, compute“train a new large language model from scratch”
IInteresting: to you and to the fielda toy problem nobody uses
NNovel: confirms, extends or challenges earlier findingsit repeats a tutorial exactly
EEthical: no harm, consent, privacy respectedscraping private health data without consent
RRelevant: matters for science, users or societythe answer would change nothing

FINER comes from clinical research and works well for computing too.

Weak and strong questions

WeakWhyStrong

Make a better spam filter.a task, not a question; “better” is undefinedDoes adding URL features reduce false positives of an SMS spam classifier at equal recall?

Study students’ use of ChatGPT.no question, no populationHow do CSE undergraduates at one university use AI assistants for programming tasks, and which uses do they consider dishonest?

Part 9 of 11

Hypotheses, objectives and contributions

Hypothesis

A testable prediction: “Method A will have higher F1 than B on dataset D.” Experiments either support or reject it. Not every thesis needs one (design and exploratory studies often do not).

Objectives

The concrete steps to answer the questions: collect…, implement…, compare…, analyse…. Make them SMART: specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-bound.

Contributions are written last, as results: “We release a dataset of…”, “We show that…”, “We propose… which improves… by…”.

Scoping and agreeing

Scoping: small enough to finish

  • Fix one main task, dataset family and metric.
  • Prefer depth (one problem studied well) to breadth (five problems done badly).
  • Write down what is out of scope.
  • Plan a minimum result (must have) and stretch goals (nice to have).

Example

Too big: “AI for Bangladeshi agriculture.”
Still big: “Detect diseases of all crops from images.”
Right size: “Detect five common rice-leaf diseases from phone photos; compare three CNNs; test robustness to field lighting.”

Scoping and agreeing

Getting the topic agreed

  1. Shortlisttwo or three topics, scored
  2. One-page ideaproblem, question, data, method, why it matters
  3. Meetdiscuss with a supervisor
  4. Read5–10 key papers on the chosen topic
  5. Refinerewrite the question
  6. Agreetopic and plan confirmed in writing

The one-page idea is your first research document. Keep it: it becomes the start of your proposal.

Worked example 1 of 3

Worked example 1

Narrow the interest “cybersecurity” to a research question for a one-year thesis.
Show the solution
Area: cybersecurity → topic: phishing detection → problem: URL-based detectors trained on English sites miss phishing pages in Bangla → question: “Do character-level URL models trained on global phishing data detect Bangla-language phishing sites, and does adding page-text features improve recall?” → objectives: collect 3,000 labelled Bangla URLs/pages, train two models, compare recall at a fixed false-positive rate, analyse misses.

Worked example 2 of 3

Worked example 2

Apply FINER to: “Build a general AI that diagnoses all diseases from medical images.”
Show the solution
F: fails — impossible data and compute. I: interesting. N: as stated, not novel enough to define. E: risky without clinical oversight and consent. R: relevant. Fix: “Detect diabetic retinopathy grade from fundus images in a public dataset, and test performance on images from a local smartphone fundus camera.”

Worked example 3 of 3

Worked example 3

Write a hypothesis and two objectives for: “Does data augmentation help a small Bangla handwritten-digit dataset?”
Show the solution
Hypothesis: “Training with rotation and elastic-distortion augmentation increases test accuracy by at least 2 percentage points compared with no augmentation.” Objectives: (1) train the same CNN five times with and without augmentation on a fixed split; (2) compare mean accuracy with a paired test and analyse which digits improve.

Practice

Practice (1 of 2)

Try each one yourself first, then check the answer.

1. Name four sources of thesis topics.
Show the answer
Your interests, the future-work sections of recent papers, local problems and data, your supervisor’s projects, industry or NGO problems, replications of published results.
2. What four parts should a precise research question name?
Show the answer
The population or data, the intervention or method, the comparison, and the outcome (how success is measured).
3. Expand FINER.
Show the answer
Feasible, Interesting, Novel, Ethical, Relevant.

Practice

Practice (2 of 2)

Try each one yourself first, then check the answer.

4. Improve: “Can machine learning predict exam results?”
Show the answer
For example: “Do features from LMS activity in the first four weeks predict which students will fail a course better than prior GPA alone, measured by recall of at-risk students?” — population, method, comparison and outcome are now explicit.
5. What is the difference between an objective and a contribution?
Show the answer
An objective is a planned step (“compare three models”); a contribution is a result you can claim afterwards (“we show that model A is 4 points better on code-mixed text”).
6. Why write down what is out of scope?
Show the answer
It protects the plan from growing, tells examiners you made a deliberate choice, and gives you natural future work.

Summary

Summary

  • Topics come from interests, recent papers’ future work, local problems, supervisors, industry and replications.
  • Narrow step by step: area → topic → problem → research question → hypothesis → objectives.
  • A good research question names the data, the method, the comparison and the outcome.
  • Check it with FINER: feasible, interesting, novel, ethical, relevant.
  • Scope tightly: one main task and metric; write down what is out of scope.
  • Secure the data and agree the topic with your supervisor in writing.
