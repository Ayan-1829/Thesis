Before we start

Why this matters

Two months of work: your model beats the baseline by 0.4%. The examiner asks: “Would it still win with a different random seed?” You check. It doesn’t.

Experiments are only as convincing as their setup: clean splits, no leakage, several runs, the right metrics and a test of whether differences are real.

This chapter turns “I trained a model” into “I can show, with evidence, that the method works — and why”.

Section 1 of 7

Getting and preparing data

  • Public datasets: cite the paper that introduced them; check the licence.
  • Your own data: plan collection, consent and storage (chapter 9); write a labelling guide.
  • Labelling: at least two annotators on a sample; report agreement (Cohen’s κ).
  • Clean: remove duplicates and broken items; document every rule.
  • Describe: size, classes, class balance, sources, time period.

A datasheet for your data

Who collected it, when, how, for what purpose; what each label means; known gaps and biases; licence and how to access it. Put it in an appendix.

Section 1 of 7

Train, validation and test

  • Train: the model learns from it.
  • Validation: you tune hyperparameters and choose models with it.
  • Test: used once, at the end, to report the result.
  • Split before any analysis; keep class balance (stratify).
  • For small data use k-fold cross-validation: every item is tested exactly once.

Leakage makes results look excellent and then fail in practice. Kapoor and Narayanan (2023) found leakage errors in hundreds of published studies across many fields that use machine learning — see the Princeton leakage catalogue.

Section 3 of 7

Running experiments

  • Start with the simplest baseline end to end; then add complexity.
  • Change one thing at a time and record it.
  • Fix and record random seeds; run each setting at least 5 times (10 if you may need the Wilcoxon test).
  • Give every method the same tuning budget.
  • Log every run: config, commit hash, metrics, time (a spreadsheet, or MLflow / Weights & Biases).

Section 5 of 7

What a p-value says

Suppose A and B were really equally good. The p-value is how often a difference at least as large as the one you saw would appear just from random variation. A small p means your result would be surprising if there were no real difference.

p < 0.05 does not mean…because…
“there is a 95% chance A is better”p assumes no difference; it is not the probability of a hypothesis
“the difference is large”with enough data a tiny difference becomes significant
“p = 0.20, so A and B are equal”a non-significant result is no evidence either way, not proof of equality
Report three things: the size of the difference, its 95% confidence interval and the p-value with the test used. “A beat B by 2.0 F1 points (95% CI 1.1 to 2.9; paired t-test over 5 seeds, p = 0.003).”

Section 5 of 7

Choose the test by what one pair is

Your pairs are…UseWatch out
seeds on one fixed splitpaired t-test; Wilcoxon signed-rank if far from normalshows only training randomness, not how the score varies with other test data
folds of one (repeated) cross-validationcorrected resampled t-test (Nadeau and Bengio), or the 5×2 cv testfolds share most training data, so the ordinary paired t-test and Wilcoxon overstate the evidence
separate, independent test sets or datasetspaired t-test or Wilcoxon; with many datasets, Friedman + post-hocuse at least 6 pairs for Wilcoxon
two classifiers on one test setMcNemar’s test, plus a bootstrap confidence interval over the test itemsone split only: say so
With 5 pairs the Wilcoxon test can never give p < 0.05 — its smallest possible p is 2 ÷ 25 = 0.0625. Plan the number of runs before you choose the test.

Section 5 of 7

Beyond p: size, uncertainty, many comparisons

Effect size

Give the difference in the metric’s own units (“+2.0 F1 points”), and if useful a standardised size such as Cohen’s d.

Confidence interval

The range of differences compatible with your data. A bootstrap interval: resample the test items with replacement 1,000 times, recompute the difference, take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles.

Many comparisons

Testing your method against 6 baselines at 0.05 gives about a 26% chance of at least one false “win”. Correct with Holm–Bonferroni.

Assumptions

The t-test assumes roughly normal differences, which you cannot check with 5 values. More runs help more than any test.

Section 6 of 7

Presenting results

  • A main results table: methods × metrics, mean ± sd, best in bold, the test, its p-value and a confidence interval noted.
  • Plots: learning curves, per-class bars, confusion matrices, examples.
  • Every figure and table numbered, captioned, and discussed in the text.
  • Say what the numbers mean, not only what they are.

Worked example 1 of 4

Worked example 1

A model for detecting pneumonia is trained and tested on X-ray images split randomly. Many patients have several images. Is there a problem?
Show the solution
Yes: images of the same patient appear in both train and test, so the model can recognise the patient rather than the disease — leakage. Split by patient ID so that each patient’s images are all in one split, and report the result again.

Worked example 2 of 4

Worked example 2

Confusion matrix for “phishing” as positive: TP = 90, FN = 10, FP = 30, TN = 870. Compute precision, recall, F1 and accuracy.
Show the solution
Precision = 90 ÷ (90 + 30) = 0.75. Recall = 90 ÷ (90 + 10) = 0.90. F1 = 2 × 0.75 × 0.90 ÷ 1.65 = 0.818. Accuracy = (90 + 870) ÷ 1000 = 0.96. Accuracy looks excellent; precision shows one in four alarms is false.

Worked example 3 of 4

Worked example 3

Method A: F1 over 5 seeds 0.80, 0.82, 0.81, 0.79, 0.83. Method B on the same seeds: 0.78, 0.80, 0.80, 0.77, 0.80. How do you report and test it?
Show the solution
Means: A 0.810 ± 0.016, B 0.790 ± 0.014. Differences A − B: 0.02, 0.02, 0.01, 0.02, 0.03; mean 0.02, sd 0.0071; t = 0.02 ÷ (0.0071 ÷ √5) ≈ 6.3 with 4 degrees of freedom, p ≈ 0.003. 95% confidence interval: 0.02 ± 2.776 × 0.0071 ÷ √5 = [0.011, 0.029]. Report: “A beat B by 2.0 F1 points (95% CI 1.1 to 2.9; paired t-test over 5 seeds, p = 0.003).” Two cautions: the seeds share one test set, so this shows the gain is not training luck, not that it holds on other data; and a Wilcoxon test on these 5 pairs gives p = 0.0625, its smallest possible value, so use more seeds if you need it.

Worked example 4 of 4

Worked example 4

Two models are compared with 10-fold cross-validation. The fold differences A − B are 0.020, −0.005, 0.010, 0.015, −0.010, 0.020, 0.005, 0.000, 0.018, 0.007. Is A better?
Show the solution
Mean difference 0.008, sd 0.0106. An ordinary paired t-test gives t = 0.008 ÷ (0.0106 ÷ √10) = 2.39, p = 0.041 — apparently significant. But each pair of folds shares 8 of its 9 training parts, so the differences are not independent. The corrected resampled t-test (Nadeau and Bengio) multiplies the variance by (1/n + ntest/ntrain) = 1/10 + 1/9 instead of 1/10: standard error = 0.0106 × √0.211 = 0.0049, t = 1.65, df = 9, p = 0.13. Conclusion: no reliable evidence that A is better. Try it in the calculator with “Cross-validation folds” selected.

Practice

Practice (1 of 2)

Try each one yourself first, then check the answer.

1. What is each split for: train, validation, test?
Show the answer
Train: fit the model. Validation: tune and select models. Test: report the final result, used once.
2. Give three examples of data leakage.
Show the answer
Duplicates across splits; the same patient/user in train and test; random splits of time series; normalisation fitted on all data; tuning on the test set.
3. Why run each experiment with several seeds?
Show the answer
Results vary with initialisation and data order; several runs give a mean and standard deviation and allow a significance test.

Practice

Practice (2 of 2)

Try each one yourself first, then check the answer.

4. Which test compares two methods evaluated on the same 10 folds of one cross-validation, and why not the ordinary paired t-test?
Show the answer
The corrected resampled t-test (Nadeau and Bengio), or the 5×2 cv test. The folds share most of their training data, so their differences are not independent; the ordinary paired t-test — and the Wilcoxon test — treat them as independent and overstate the evidence.
5. What is the difference between an ablation study and error analysis?
Show the answer
Ablation removes components to measure their contribution; error analysis studies the mistakes to understand why the method fails.
6. Why can a Wilcoxon signed-rank test on 5 seeds never be significant at 0.05?
Show the answer
With 5 pairs there are only 25 = 32 equally likely sign patterns; even when all 5 differences favour A, the two-sided p is 2 ÷ 32 = 0.0625. Use at least 6 pairs, or the t-test if the differences look roughly normal.

Summary

Summary

  • Describe and document your data; measure label agreement; check licences.
  • Split into train, validation and test (or use cross-validation); test once, at the end.
  • Prevent leakage: no duplicates or subjects across splits, time-based splits, fit pre-processing on train only.
  • Run several seeds, log every run, give every method the same tuning budget.
  • Report the size of a difference with its confidence interval, and test it with a method that matches what one pair is (seeds, folds, test sets); add ablation and error analysis.
  • Make every number reproducible: Git, environment, seeds, scripts, README.
