Before we start

Why this matters

Excellent experiments, poorly written, read as a weak thesis. Examiners can only judge what they can understand.

Writing is not a final step: it is how you organise your thinking and show your evidence. A clear structure, a strong introduction and well-explained results can lift a thesis by a whole grade.

This chapter covers the structure of each chapter, the abstract, academic style, figures, LaTeX and references, and a writing process that finishes on time.

Structure

Front and back matter

Before chapter 1

  1. Title page (title, authors and IDs, supervisor, department, date)
  2. Declaration of originality, signed
  3. Approval page
  4. Acknowledgements
  5. Abstract
  6. Table of contents, lists of figures and tables, abbreviations

After the last chapter

  1. References, in one consistent style
  2. Appendices: extra results, questionnaires, consent forms, datasheet, code listings

Follow your department’s template for margins, fonts and page order; the milestones page has a LaTeX starting point.

Part 3 of 11

Writing the introduction

  1. Contextthe field and why it matters
  2. Problemwhat is difficult or missing — with evidence
  3. Gapwhat existing work does not do
  4. Research questionswhat exactly you ask
  5. Contributionswhat you deliver, as a bulleted list
  6. Outlineone line per chapter

Readers decide in the introduction whether your thesis is worth reading. The contributions list should be checkable: “We release a dataset of 5,000 labelled comments”, not “we study sentiment”.

The chapters

Methodology and results chapters

Methodology

  • data: source, size, cleaning, splits
  • proposed method with a diagram
  • baselines and why
  • metrics and evaluation setup
  • implementation details (in an appendix if long)

Results and discussion

  • main results table first
  • answer each RQ in turn, with evidence
  • ablation and error analysis
  • compare with earlier work: agree or disagree, and why
  • limitations and threats to validity
Results say what happened; discussion says why and so what. Do not leave the interpretation to the reader.

Academic style

PreferAvoid
short, direct sentenceslong chains of clauses
“We trained the model for 4 epochs.”“The model was trained by us for a duration of four epochs.”
precise numbers: “improved macro-F1 by 3.1 points”“significantly better” without a test
one term for one thing, defined onceswitching between “dataset”, “corpus” and “data collection”
hedging where evidence is limited: “suggests”, “may”“proves”, “always”, “perfect”
present tense for facts, past for what you didrandom tense changes

Both “we” and the passive are accepted in computing; be consistent and follow your supervisor’s preference.

Writing well

Figures and tables

  • Every figure and table is numbered, has a caption, and is referred to in the text before it appears.
  • The caption says what to notice: “Our model (blue) needs half the training data to reach the baseline’s F1.”
  • Label axes with units; use readable font sizes; colour-blind-safe colours.
  • Tables: best values in bold, mean ± sd, units in headers.
  • Prefer vector graphics (PDF/SVG) for plots and diagrams.
A reader skimming only the figures and captions should understand your main result.

LaTeX and references

References

  • Cite every idea, method, dataset, figure and quotation that is not yours.
  • Use one style (IEEE numeric is common in computing) through the whole thesis.
  • Let the tools format it: Zotero → BibTeX → \cite{key} → \printbibliography.
  • Check every entry: authors, title, venue, year, pages, DOI. Remove arXiv versions when a published version exists.

IEEE style

[1] S. Keshav, “How to read a paper,” ACM SIGCOMM Comput. Commun. Rev., vol. 37, no. 3, pp. 83–84, 2007.
[2] J. Devlin, M.-W. Chang, K. Lee, and K. Toutanova, “BERT: Pre-training of deep bidirectional transformers for language understanding,” in Proc. NAACL-HLT, 2019, pp. 4171–4186.

The writing process

  1. Outlineheadings + one sentence each, agreed with the supervisor
  2. Draftwrite fast; do not polish yet
  3. Figures firstthe story is in the results
  4. Revisestructure, then paragraphs, then sentences
  5. Feedbacksupervisor and a classmate
  6. Checkreferences, numbering, similarity report
  7. Proofreadon paper or read aloud

Plan about three months for writing, with drafts sent chapter by chapter. Write the methodology and results first, the introduction and abstract last.

Process and feedback

Handling supervisor feedback

  • Read all comments before changing anything; group them by type.
  • Fix the big ones (structure, missing analysis) before the small ones (wording).
  • Reply in a short list: comment → what you changed (or why not).
  • Do not argue in silence: if you disagree, explain with evidence.
  • Send the next version with changes highlighted (latexdiff, or Word track changes).

Worked example 3

Rewrite: “It was found by us that the accuracy of the proposed model was significantly better.”
Show the solution
“Our model improved accuracy from 84.2% to 86.9% (mean of five runs; p = 0.01, paired t-test).” — active, precise, and “significantly” is backed by a test.

Practice (2 of 2)

Try each one yourself first, then check the answer.

4. What is the difference between results and discussion?
Show the answer
Results report what happened (numbers, tables, figures); discussion explains why, compares with earlier work, and states the implications and limitations.
5. Three rules for figures and tables.
Show the answer
Numbered and captioned; referred to in the text before they appear; readable labels with units (and best values marked in tables).
6. Which chapters should you write first, and which last?
Show the answer
Methodology and results first (they are what you did); introduction, conclusion and abstract last (they describe what you found).

Summary

Summary

  • A thesis follows introduction, literature review, methodology, results and discussion, conclusion — plus front and back matter.
  • The introduction gives context, problem, gap, questions, contributions and an outline.
  • Results say what happened; discussion says why and so what, including limitations.
  • Write clearly and precisely; back every claim with numbers or tests.
  • Number, caption and refer to every figure and table; use one reference style via BibTeX.
  • Outline, draft, revise with feedback, check and proofread; write the abstract last.
