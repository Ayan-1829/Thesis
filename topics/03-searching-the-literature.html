Before we start

Why this matters

Three months into your brilliant new method, you find a paper from last year that did exactly the same thing. Better. With a nicer figure.

A systematic search prevents that heartbreak and hands you the datasets, baselines and metrics you need. It also shows examiners that your claim of novelty rests on evidence.

This chapter shows where to search, how to write search strings, how to follow citations and how to record what you did.

Part 1 of 10

Why search systematically

A literature search is not “type a few words into Google”. You need to find the important papers, the recent ones and the ones that do almost what you plan — before your examiners do.

  • It shows what is already known, so you do not reinvent it.
  • It gives you baselines, datasets and metrics.
  • It reveals the gap your thesis fills.
  • A recorded search can be repeated and defended.

Part 4 of 10

Building a search string

  1. Write the RQ as concepts: e.g. sentiment · Bangla · social media.
  2. For each concept list synonyms: Bangla / Bengali; sentiment / opinion / emotion; social media / Facebook / YouTube comments.
  3. Join synonyms with OR inside brackets, concepts with AND.
  4. Use "quotes" for exact phrases and * for word endings where supported.
  5. Run it, read the first results, refine the words — and record each version.
("Bangla" OR "Bengali")
AND (sentiment OR opinion OR emotion)
AND ("social media" OR Facebook OR YouTube)

Google Scholar ignores brackets and wildcards in some cases; use fewer, more specific terms there, and the full string in IEEE Xplore, ACM, Scopus.

Snowballing

Part 7 of 10

Snowballing (cont.)

Start from a few strong “seed” papers and follow citations both ways:

  • Backward: read the reference list of the seed — what it builds on.
  • Forward: use “Cited by” in Google Scholar or Semantic Scholar — who built on it since.
  • Repeat with the best new papers until no new relevant papers appear.
Keyword search plus snowballing finds far more of the relevant work than either alone.

Following citations

Getting the full text legally

  1. Open the link from the university network or library proxy (IEEE, ACM, Springer, Elsevier).
  2. Look for an open-access version: arXiv, the author’s homepage, a university repository, Unpaywall.
  3. Check ResearchGate or the author’s profile for an accepted version.
  4. Email the author politely — most are happy to send their paper.
  5. Ask your supervisor or the library for an inter-library request.
Do not use pirate sites. Besides being illegal, they often serve wrong versions and malware.

Recording the search

How much is enough?

  • You keep meeting the same papers and authors — the search is saturating.
  • You can name the 3–5 most important papers and the current best result.
  • You know the standard datasets, baselines and metrics.
  • You can state the gap in one paragraph.

Typical numbers

Screen a few hundred titles, read 40–80 abstracts, read 20–40 papers properly, and cite 30–60 in an undergraduate thesis. Quality beats quantity.

Worked example 1 of 2

Worked example 1

Turn the RQ “Does data augmentation improve rice-leaf disease classification on smartphone photos?” into a search string.
Show the solution
Concepts and synonyms: (rice OR paddy) · (leaf disease OR plant disease) · (classification OR detection OR recognition) · (augmentation OR “synthetic data”). String: (rice OR paddy) AND ("leaf disease" OR "plant disease") AND (classif* OR detect* OR recogni*) AND (augmentation OR "synthetic data"). In Google Scholar, simplify to: rice leaf disease detection augmentation smartphone.

Worked example 2 of 2

Worked example 2

A seed paper has 45 references and is cited by 120 papers. How do you snowball efficiently?
Show the solution
Backward: scan the 45 titles, keep those on your problem (say 8). Forward: in “Cited by”, sort by date or search within citing articles for your keywords; scan titles of the 120, keep about 10. Read abstracts of the 18 and repeat from the 3–4 best. Record each step in the search log.

Practice

Practice (1 of 2)

Try each one yourself first, then check the answer.

1. Name three databases and say what each is best for.
Show the answer
For example: Google Scholar (widest coverage and citations), IEEE Xplore (networks, signal processing, IoT), arXiv (the newest ML/AI preprints); also ACM DL, DBLP, Semantic Scholar, PubMed.
2. What do AND, OR and quotes do in a search string?
Show the answer
AND requires all concepts (narrower); OR allows any synonym (wider); quotes keep an exact phrase.
3. Explain backward and forward snowballing.
Show the answer
Backward: follow a paper’s reference list to older work. Forward: follow “Cited by” to newer papers that build on it.

Show the answer
It has not necessarily been peer-reviewed; check whether a reviewed version was later published, and read its claims critically.

University library access or proxy; arXiv or other open-access copy; author homepage or repository (Unpaywall finds it); emailing the author; inter-library request.

Show the answer
Date, database, exact query, filters, number of results, number kept — so the search can be repeated and described in the thesis.

Summary

Summary

  • Search systematically: it finds the key, recent and closest work, plus datasets, baselines and metrics.
  • Use several sources: Google Scholar, Semantic Scholar, IEEE Xplore, ACM DL, arXiv, DBLP (PubMed for biomedicine).
  • Build strings from concepts and synonyms: OR within a concept, AND between concepts, quotes for phrases.
  • Snowball backward (references) and forward (“Cited by”) from strong seed papers.
  • Get full text legally: library access, open-access copies, authors.
  • Keep a search log; stop when results saturate and you can state the gap.
